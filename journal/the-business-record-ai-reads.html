Every place your business is described, from your site to a maps profile to a directory to a booking platform, is a source. A model reading them does not average conflicting facts and does not assume the newest one is right. It either states nothing about that detail, or it prefers a business whose sources agree. Consistency is not tidiness. It is the cheapest reliability signal you own.

What the record is made of

Four facts do most of the work, because they are the four an AI assistant is asked for most often.

Name. The exact trading name, in one form. Not Reptify in one place and Reptify Media LLC in another and Reptify Media & Co. on a directory somebody filled in six years ago.

Address. One format, including whether the suite number sits on its own line and whether it is spelled out or abbreviated. If you have no walk-in premises, say so plainly rather than implying one.

Contact. One number that is actually answered, and one email. A number that rings out is worse than no number, because it converts a good recommendation into a bad experience.

What you do and where. The services, named the way a customer would name them, and the area you actually serve. This is the field businesses most often leave to inference, and inference is where you lose.

None of this is an argument you have to take on trust. Google, setting out how local results are ranked, puts it in one line: “Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results. If your business info isn’t accurate, your Business Profile might not show up for relevant searches in your area.”

Why disagreement costs more than silence

Assistants are built to avoid asserting things they cannot support. Give one two different sets of hours and it does the safe thing. It hedges, or it names a business it can describe without hedging.

The damage is not limited to the field in question. A model that finds your hours unreliable treats the rest of your record as less reliable too. One stale listing can quietly discount everything else you have published.

This is why consistency beats volume. Ten listings that agree are worth more than forty that mostly do.

The stale listing problem

Businesses that have been trading a while tend to accumulate these, and the longer the trading history the more of them there are:

A directory created by a former employee, on an email nobody can access. An old suite number from before the move. A former trading name. A phone number that forwarded to a service you cancelled. An aggregator that scraped a competitor’s category and attached it to you.

You do not need to find all of them. You need to find the ones that rank, which is to say the sources an AI assistant actually cites when it describes you. Those are visible in the citation list of any assistant that shows its sources, and that is what makes this job finite instead of endless. Ask three AI assistants about your business (ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, say) and write down every source they name, and you have your list.

A note on addresses with no front door

A great many businesses have no counter for anyone to walk up to. A landscaping crew running out of two trucks across three counties. A bookkeeper at a kitchen table. A consultancy that only ever meets clients in the client’s own boardroom. A mobile detailer, a wedding photographer, a winery with no tasting room. If that is you, the address question has one right answer and one very tempting wrong one.

Take a worked example, and take it as exactly that rather than as anyone’s real listing. Suppose a plumber covers four towns from a workshop attached to his house. He wants to appear in local results, so he puts the house on the map, sets opening hours of eight to six, and writes come and see us on the contact page. Every one of those three decisions is a problem, and none of them is the one he thinks he is making.

Google’s Business Profile guidelines are direct about the eligibility test. A location must “receive customers at the location during business hours” and be “staffed during business hours.” They rule out “P.O. boxes or mailboxes located at remote locations.” And on the arrangement people most often assume is fine: “if your business rents a physical mailing address but doesn’t operate out of that location, also known as a virtual office, that location isn’t eligible for a Business Profile.”

Our plumber staffs no counter, so the hours are a claim he cannot keep, the pin invites strangers to his family’s door, and come and see us contradicts what happens if anyone does. He has manufactured three disagreements about himself in an afternoon, which is the thing this whole article is about.

The move instead is unglamorous and it is written down. Publish a postal address and a service area, and skip the pin, the hours and the storefront language. Google’s instruction for this case leaves little room: “if you’re a service-area business, you should hide your business address from customers.”

Get it wrong and the downside is not that a model hedges about you. Google reserves the right to suspend profiles that break these rules, and a suspension usually arrives long after the reviews have accumulated on it. The listing goes, and the reviews go with it.

What you give up is a pin on a map. What you keep is a record that holds together under exactly the checking described here. A firm working out of a van, a home office or a client’s boardroom is not a less serious firm. A great many of the businesses worth hiring have never had a counter, and the ones that do rarely win the work because of it.

Making them agree, in order

Write the canonical version down. One document with the exact name, address, phone, email, service list and service area. Every future listing gets copied from it. This takes twenty minutes and prevents the next five years of drift.

Fix your own site first. It is the source you fully control and the one others copy from. Check the footer against the contact page against the schema against the booking tool — they disagree more often than owners expect.

Then the cited sources. Whatever the AI assistants actually quote, in the order they quote it.

Then the rest, slowly. The long tail matters less than the effort suggests. Do not let it become the project.

One name, one address, one description of what you do. It is unglamorous, it is the highest-yield work available, and it is the foundation the rest of AI Visibility Strategy is built on.

Key points

  • Four facts carry the record: name, address, contact, and what you do and where.
  • A model that finds one field unreliable discounts your whole record.
  • Ten agreeing listings beat forty that mostly agree.
  • Ask three AI assistants about your business (ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, say) and note every source they cite. That list, not the whole web, is the job.
  • Google states that a virtual office is not eligible for a Business Profile. Publish postal plus service area, never a storefront.

Questions people ask

How many directories does my business need?

Fewer than most people are sold. A handful of relevant, accurate listings beat dozens of scraped ones. Relevance and agreement are what count, not volume.

My old business name still appears everywhere. Does that hurt?

Yes, and it is worth fixing at the sources that get cited. A model seeing two names either hedges about your identity or attaches your reviews to the wrong entity.

I work from home. What address should I publish?

Publish a service area and a way to contact you, and do not publish a private residence as a storefront. Google’s guidance for service-area businesses is to hide the address from customers outright. If you need a postal address for correspondence, use one and describe it as postal rather than as a place customers visit.

Does an address change reset my visibility?

It disrupts it while the sources catch up, and the disruption lasts longer if you only update some of them. Change the canonical document first, then work through the cited sources in order.
